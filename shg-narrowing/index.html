Spectral narrowing by SHG in BBO

Type-I second-harmonic generation (SHG) in a long β-BaB2O4 (BBO) crystal narrows the spectrum: the second harmonic (SH) travels slower than the fundamental (FF), so only a narrow band of SH frequencies builds up over the crystal. This is the spectral-compression step that makes narrowband Raman pump pulses for femtosecond stimulated Raman spectroscopy (FSRS). Enter the FF wavelength, the crystal length and the FF pulse. The page gives the SH width in cm⁻¹, nm and GHz from an undepleted model with the exact BBO dispersion, shows the SH spectrum and pulse, compares the width with an etalon, and checks whether pump depletion or walk-off matter. Everything recalculates as you type. Equations are at the bottom.

Intensities normalised to 1 at their peaks. Time runs in a frame that moves with the mean of the FF and SH group velocities; later times are to the right. The FF leaves the crystal first, at −GDM/2; SH made at the entrance face comes out last, at +GDM/2, so a long crystal gives a flat-top SH pulse about GDM long. This is the undepleted shape: with strong conversion the FF is used up as it goes, and the real pulse has a steeper rise and an exponential tail, which this page does not model. With short pulses (try 800 nm, 35 fs) the FF also stretches by its own dispersion inside the crystal, so most of the SH is made in the first millimetres and comes out at the late edge; the ripples there are SH from different depths adding with different phases as the FF chirps (they do not change when the grids are refined).

The depletion flag assumes ideal phase matching, a Gaussian beam and pulse, and the deff entered. Real efficiencies are usually lower, so the flag errs on the side of warning. Its levels come from split-step simulations with depletion (1110 nm, 200 fs; see equation 11).

Methods and equations

Type-I (ooe) SHG: the FF is an ordinary wave at λ, the SH an extraordinary wave at λ/2. BBO is negative uniaxial. λ is in µm in the Sellmeier equations. Coupled-wave conventions follow Boyd, Nonlinear Optics.

  1. Refractive indices (Sellmeier equations)

    Kato 1986: no² = 2.7359 + 0.01878/(λ² − 0.01822) − 0.01354 λ²,  ne² = 2.3753 + 0.01224/(λ² − 0.01667) − 0.01516 λ²
    Eimerl et al. 1987: no² = 2.7405 + 0.0184/(λ² − 0.0179) − 0.0155 λ²,  ne² = 2.3730 + 0.0128/(λ² − 0.0156) − 0.0044 λ²
    Tamošauskas et al. 2018: no² = 1 + 0.90291 λ²/(λ² − 0.003926) + 0.83155 λ²/(λ² − 0.018786) + 0.76536 λ²/(λ² − 60.01),
    ne² = 1 + 1.151075 λ²/(λ² − 0.007142) + 0.21803 λ²/(λ² − 0.02259) + 0.656 λ²/(λ² − 263)

    Fitted ranges: Kato 0.205–1.064 µm, Eimerl 0.22–1.06 µm, Tamošauskas 0.188–5.2 µm. Eimerl and Tamošauskas coefficients as in the refractiveindex.info database; Kato's as in vendor data sheets. Kato's set is the default. Above 1.064 µm Kato and Eimerl are extrapolated. Kato still gives a GVM within about 3 % of Tamošauskas up to 1.3 µm, but Eimerl's GVM drifts away: 9 % low at 1110 nm, 22 % low at 1300 nm.

  2. Extraordinary index at angle θ to the optic axis

    1/ne(θ, λ)² = cos²θ/no(λ)² + sin²θ/ne(λ)²

  3. Type-I phase matching

    ne(θpm, λ/2) = no(λ) ⇒ sin²θpm = [no(λ)⁻² − no(λ/2)⁻²] / [ne(λ/2)⁻² − no(λ/2)⁻²]
    sin θext = no(λ) sin(θpm − θcut)

    There is no solution when the right side is above 1: below 409.5 nm FF with Kato's equations (409.0 nm Eimerl, 409.9 nm Tamošauskas). θext is the tilt of the crystal face from normal incidence that reaches θpm with a crystal cut at θcut.

  4. Group index, group-velocity mismatch (GVM) and group-delay mismatch (GDM)

    ng = n − λ dn/dλ,  GVM = 1/vg,SH − 1/vg,FF = (ng,SH − ng,FF)/c,  GDM = L·|GVM|

    ng,FF from no(λ), ng,SH from ne(θpm, λ/2) with θ held fixed; the derivatives are analytic. GVM is positive (the SH is slower) for FF below about 1.5 µm and crosses zero at 1541 nm (Kato), 1471 nm (Eimerl) or 1518 nm (Tamošauskas). Below, δ = −GVM.

  5. Simple bandwidth formula (GVM only, FF much broader than the acceptance)

    Δk ≈ δ·Ω,  S(Ω) ∝ sinc²(δΩL/2),  sinc²(1.39156) = ½ ⇒  ΔνSH = 2·1.39156/(π·GDM) = 0.886/GDM
    Δν̃ = Δν/(100 c) in cm⁻¹,  Δλ = λ² Δν/c,  thin crystal: ΔνSH = √2·ΔνFF

    Ω is the SH detuning; summed over the pairs of FF frequencies that add up to it, the mismatch depends on Ω alone. This is a lower bound on the width: it fails when GDM is short compared with the FF pulse (thin crystals), and when δ → 0 near 1.5 µm. "GVM only" on the spectrum plot is sinc²(δΩL/2)·exp(−Ω²/4σ²), the same with the finite FF spectrum. Nanometre widths use the linear conversion Δλ = λ²Δν/c.

  6. Full undepleted model

    A(Ω₁) = exp(−Ω₁²/4σ² + i·GDD·Ω₁²/2),  σ = √(2 ln 2)/τ,  ΔνFF = 0.4413/τ
    Δk(Ω, Ω₁) = ke(2ω₀ + Ω; θpm) − ko(ω₀ + Ω₁) − ko(ω₀ + Ω − Ω₁),  k = n(ω) ω/c
    ESH(Ω) ∝ ∫ A(Ω₁) A(Ω − Ω₁) · L sinc(ΔkL/2) e−iΔkL/2 dΩ₁,  S(Ω) = |ESH(Ω)|²

    τ is the intensity FWHM of the transform-limited FF pulse, and GDD > 0 is the sign that normal glass adds. θ is held at θpm of the centre wavelength, and the indices are the exact Sellmeier values, not a Taylor expansion, so the model holds GVM, the FF dispersion (which sets the FF acceptance bandwidth, Marangoni et al. 2007), the chirp and the finite FF bandwidth. With Ω₁ = Ω/2 ± u the integrand is even in u; the page integrates u from 0 to 6σ by the trapezoid rule, with steps that resolve the phases GDD·u² and k″Lu², and finds the FWHM on an adaptive grid with 40–80 points across the peak. Limits: L → 0 gives √2·ΔνFF; a long crystal gives the sinc² of equation 5. The FWHM is taken around the highest point, out to the first half-maximum crossing on each side.

    Without a phase-matching filter a linear chirp factors out of A(Ω₁)A(Ω − Ω₁), so the thin-crystal SH spectrum does not depend on it. In a long crystal the chirp acts only through the FF dispersion: for 200 fs at 1110 nm, ±20000 fs² changes the 25 mm width by less than 0.01 %; for 35 fs at 800 nm, −1000 fs² (the FF then compresses halfway through the crystal) broadens it from 6.17 to 6.51 cm⁻¹.

  7. SH pulse in time

    ESH(t) = ∫ ESH(Ω) ei[ke(2ω₀+Ω) − ke(2ω₀) − k̄′Ω]L + i·GDD·Ω²/4 e−iΩt dΩ,  k̄′ = (ng,FF + ng,SH)/2c
    GVM only: ESH(t) ∝ ∫₀L A₁²(t − δ(z − L/2)) dz

    A direct Fourier sum over the computed spectrum; the SH keeps its own dispersion and the chirp it inherits from the FF. The SH pulse FWHM in the table is the FWHM of |ESH(t)|². The GVM-only pulse uses the chirped input FF and no dispersion inside the crystal. The FF at the exit is the input pulse with the added GDD k″oL of the crystal, at the time δL/2.

  8. Spatial walk-off

    tan ρ = ½ ne(θ)² [ne⁻² − no⁻²] sin 2θ at λ/2,  walk-off over the crystal = L tan ρ,  w = d/√(2 ln 2) = 0.849 d

    Not part of the bandwidth. w is the 1/e² intensity radius of a Gaussian beam with intensity FWHM d. The page flags a walk-off larger than w, and notes one larger than w/2. Walk-off shortens the length over which the SH builds up, which broadens the SH and weakens the depletion compared with the plane-wave numbers here.

  9. Etalon

    T(ν̃) = 1/[1 + F sin²(πν̃/FSR)],  F = 1/sin²(π·δν̃/(2·FSR))

    An ideal lossless Airy etalon with free spectral range FSR and line width δν̃ (FWHM), with one order centred on the SH peak. The table gives the FWHM of what passes, the fraction of the SH energy in that order and in the other orders. Real etalons transmit less at the peak.

  10. Peak intensity and the iris measurement

    I₀ = 2√(ln 2/π) E/τ × 4 ln 2/(πd²) ≈ 0.829 E/(τd²),  τchirped = τ √[1 + (4 ln 2·GDD/τ²)²]
    fraction through an iris of diameter D = 1 − exp(−4 ln 2·(D/2)²/d²) = ½ at D = d

    A pulse Gaussian in time (intensity FWHM τ) and space (intensity FWHM d). For 2 µJ, 200 fs and 2 mm, I₀ = 2.07 × 10⁸ W/cm². The table shows I₀ of the chirped pulse.

  11. Undepleted efficiency and the depletion flag

    κ = 2ω₁deff/(n c),  I = 2nε₀c|A|²,  ESH(Ω) = (iκ/2π) ∫ Ã(Ω₁) Ã(Ω − Ω₁) L sinc(ΔkL/2) e−iΔkL/2 dΩ₁
    ηpeak = ∫|ESH|² dΩ / ∫|Ã|² dΩ,  ηbeam = ηpeak/2

    Equation 6 made absolute, with A(t) = (1/2π)∫Ã(Ω) e−iΩt dΩ the FF field in V/m and Parseval for the energies. ηpeak is the plane-wave efficiency at the beam centre and is linear in I₀; averaged over a Gaussian beam it halves, because ∫I² dA = ½ I₀ ∫I dA. Without dispersion this is the textbook η = 8π²deff²L²I/(ε₀c n³λ²) times 1/√2 for a Gaussian pulse. It is not a real efficiency once it nears 1. The flag levels come from split-step simulations with depletion (plane wave and Gaussian beam; 1110 nm, 200 fs, deff = 2 pm/V, 2, 7 and 25 mm):

    undepleted ηbeamwhat the simulations showed
    < 0.1depletion negligible
    0.1–1depletion likely; the efficiency estimate is too high, the SH width within about 7 % (within 1 % at 25 mm; 2 mm is the worst case)
    1–10strong depletion; real efficiency saturates around 40–80 %; width still within about 7 %, slightly narrower for thin crystals, slightly broader at 25 mm
    > 10very strong depletion, possible back-conversion; the width can change by 10 % or more (25 % at the beam centre) and the spectrum can have structure

    Where GVM sets the width, depletion changes the efficiency a lot but the SH width only a little, until the conversion is very high.

Notes

Not modelled

  • Pump depletion and back-conversion. The page estimates whether depletion is likely and flags it (equation 11); it does not change the spectrum or the pulse. The steep rise and exponential tail of real long-crystal SH pulses come from depletion.
  • Focusing, the angular acceptance and diffraction: the beams are plane waves at θpm.
  • Spatial walk-off inside the model (it is only flagged), cascaded χ(2) effects, absorption and the crystal temperature.

A number in the literature to watch

Pontecorvo et al. 2013 write that the GVM of BBO goes "from 320 to 90 fs/mm when tuning the fundamental from 660 to 1500 nm". The 320 fs/mm at 660 nm reproduces with all three Sellmeier sets (315–320 fs/mm), but they all put 90 fs/mm near 1030 nm (Kato 94.8 fs/mm) and between −4 and +6 fs/mm at 1500 nm, where type-I BBO is nearly group-velocity matched. The red circles on the GVM plot mark the two quoted values.
