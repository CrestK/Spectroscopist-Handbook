1. Birefringence: two refractive indices in one crystal

Think of each electron in a crystal as tied to its atom by springs. In glass the springs are the same in every direction. In a crystal they can be stiffer along one axis than across it, so the index depends on which way E pushes the electrons. In a uniaxial crystal the odd direction is the optic axis (a direction, not a line). A wave whose E is perpendicular to the optic axis, the ordinary or o-wave, always sees no. A wave whose E lies in the plane of k and the optic axis, the extraordinary or e-wave, sees ne when it travels across the axis and an index between no and ne at other angles. Along the optic axis both see no: no birefringence. Δn = ne − no is positive for quartz and negative for calcite. The polarization that sees the smaller index travels faster; in a wave plate its direction is the fast axis.

2. Double refraction: the o-ray and the e-ray

Unpolarized light entering a birefringent crystal splits into an o-wave and an e-wave polarized at right angles. The o-wave follows Snell's law with no. The e-wave follows Snell's law for its wave normal k, but with an index that depends on the angle between k and the optic axis, and its energy (the ray) does not travel along k: it walks off by an angle ρ. Through a calcite rhomb this gives two images. With the optic axis perpendicular to the plane of incidence the e-wave sees ne in every direction in that plane, so both rays obey Snell's law, one with no and one with ne. With the optic axis along the face and light at normal incidence the two waves stay together and only their phase differs: a wave plate (part 3).

3. Inside a wave plate: how the phase difference builds up

Cut a birefringent crystal so the optic axis lies in its faces and send light in at normal incidence. The two waves then travel in the same direction with no walk-off, but at different speeds: the component along the fast axis at c/nf, the component along the slow axis at c/ns. After a thickness d the slow component has fallen behind by the retardance Γ = 2π(ns − nf)d/λ₀. A quarter-wave plate has Γ = 90° (a quarter of a period), a half-wave plate Γ = 180°. Nothing else changes: each component keeps its amplitude, only their relative phase moves.

Red: the component along the slow axis, in the vertical plane. Black arrows: the total field E along the beam. Inside the plate the slow component's wavelength is shorter, so it falls behind; after the plate the components keep the phase difference Γ they gained, and the black arrow tips trace the new polarization. The birefringence is exaggerated so the plate is only a few wavelengths thick; a real zero-order quartz quarter-wave plate is about 28 wavelengths thick (part 7).

4. Wave plates: the general equations, step by step

One matrix describes every wave plate: a plate with retardance Γ and its fast axis at θ. The quarter- and half-wave plates are Γ = π/2 and Γ = π in it. Work in Jones vectors: the column (Ex, Ey) of complex amplitudes, with the real field E = Re[(Ex, Ey) ei(kz − ωt)].

  1. The retardance. Across the thickness d the fast component gains the phase nfk₀d and the slow one nsk₀d. Only the difference matters:

    \Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}\,(n_s - n_f)\,d = \frac{2\pi\,|\Delta n|\,d}{\lambda_0}
    \begin{aligned} \text{quarter-wave:}\quad \Gamma &= \frac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi m, & d &= \left(m + \tfrac14\right)\frac{\lambda_0}{|\Delta n|} \\[6pt] \text{half-wave:}\quad \Gamma &= \pi + 2\pi m, & d &= \left(m + \tfrac12\right)\frac{\lambda_0}{|\Delta n|} \end{aligned}

    m = 0 is a zero-order plate. Adding whole waves (m ≥ 1) changes nothing at the design wavelength.

  2. In the plate's own axes. Put x′ along the fast axis and y′ along the slow axis. The plate leaves the fast component alone and delays the slow one by Γ:

    \begin{pmatrix} E_f' \\ E_s' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\Gamma} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} E_f \\ E_s \end{pmatrix}

    With ei(kz − ωt), the factor eiΓ makes the slow component reach each value a time Γ/ω later. The common phase nfk₀d is dropped: nothing measures it.

  3. Components along the plate's axes. With the fast axis at θ from x, the unit vectors along the axes are f̂ = (cos θ, sin θ) and ŝ = (−sin θ, cos θ). The components along them are dot products, a rotation by −θ:

    \begin{pmatrix} E_f \\ E_s \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} E_x \\ E_y \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf R(-\theta) \begin{pmatrix} E_x \\ E_y \end{pmatrix}

  4. Back to x and y. Rotate back by +θ. The plate's matrix in the lab is R(θ) · diag(1, eiΓ) · R(−θ):

    \mathbf J(\theta,\Gamma) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos^2\theta + e^{i\Gamma}\sin^2\theta & (1 - e^{i\Gamma})\sin\theta\cos\theta \\[4pt] (1 - e^{i\Gamma})\sin\theta\cos\theta & \sin^2\theta + e^{i\Gamma}\cos^2\theta \end{pmatrix}

  5. The same matrix in its most useful form. Take out the common phase eiΓ/2:

    \mathbf J(\theta,\Gamma) = e^{i\Gamma/2}\left[\cos\frac{\Gamma}{2}\;\mathbf 1 \;-\; i\sin\frac{\Gamma}{2}\;\mathbf M(\theta)\right]
    \mathbf M(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos 2\theta & \sin 2\theta \\ \sin 2\theta & -\cos 2\theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf M(\theta) \begin{pmatrix} \cos\alpha \\ \sin\alpha \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(2\theta - \alpha) \\ \sin(2\theta - \alpha) \end{pmatrix}

    M(θ) is the mirror in the fast axis: it sends a line at α to the line at 2θ − α. So every wave plate is a mix of "do nothing" (weight cos Γ/2) and "mirror in the fast axis" (weight sin Γ/2), a quarter period out of step. This one line is the general equation; the rest follows from it.

  6. Half-wave plate (Γ = π). cos(π/2) = 0 and eiπ/2(−i) = 1, so the plate is exactly the mirror:

    \mathbf J_{\lambda/2}(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos 2\theta & \sin 2\theta \\ \sin 2\theta & -\cos 2\theta \end{pmatrix}
    \begin{aligned} \text{linear at } \alpha &\;\longrightarrow\; \text{linear at } 2\theta - \alpha \\[5pt] \mathbf J_{\lambda/2}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \mp i \end{pmatrix} &= e^{\mp 2i\theta}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \pm i \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}

    Linear light comes out mirrored in the fast axis: at φ = α − θ from the fast axis in, at −φ out, turned by −2φ. Turning the plate by Δθ turns the light by 2Δθ. Circular light flips handedness (right ↔ left) at any θ, and any ellipse is mirrored: azimuth ψ → 2θ − ψ, ellipticity χ → −χ.

  7. Quarter-wave plate (Γ = π/2). cos(π/4) = sin(π/4) = 1/√2:

    \mathbf J_{\lambda/4}(\theta) = \frac{e^{i\pi/4}}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 - i\cos 2\theta & -i\sin 2\theta \\ -i\sin 2\theta & 1 + i\cos 2\theta \end{pmatrix}
    \mathbf J_{\lambda/4}\begin{pmatrix} \cos\alpha \\ \sin\alpha \end{pmatrix} = \frac{e^{i\pi/4}}{\sqrt2}\left[\begin{pmatrix} \cos\alpha \\ \sin\alpha \end{pmatrix} - i\begin{pmatrix} \cos(2\theta - \alpha) \\ \sin(2\theta - \alpha) \end{pmatrix}\right]

    The real field is ∝ û cos ωt − v̂ sin ωt: it swings from the input direction û to its mirror image v̂ a quarter period later, so the ellipse's axes lie along the bisectors of û and v̂, which are the fast and slow axes. In the plate's own axes, with φ = α − θ:

    \begin{pmatrix} E_f \\ E_s \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi \\ i\sin\varphi \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \frac{b}{a} = |\tan\varphi| \ \text{or}\ |\cot\varphi|

    Semi-axes |cos φ| along the fast axis and |sin φ| along the slow axis. E turns counterclockwise (left) for 0 < φ < 90° and clockwise (right) for −90° < φ < 0; φ = ±45° gives circular light, φ = 0° or 90° leaves the light unchanged. Run backward: circular light comes out linear at 45° to the axes, right-circular at θ + 45° and left-circular at θ − 45°.

  8. Any plate, linear light in. The output ellipse has azimuth ψ and ellipticity angle χ (tan |χ| = b/a, χ > 0 right-handed):

    \begin{aligned} \tan 2(\psi - \theta) &= \cos\Gamma\;\tan 2(\alpha - \theta) \\[5pt] \sin 2\chi &= -\sin\Gamma\;\sin 2(\alpha - \theta) \end{aligned}

    Γ = π gives ψ = 2θ − α and χ = 0 (step 6); Γ = π/2 gives ψ = θ or θ + 90° and χ = −(α − θ) (step 7).

  9. Any plate, any light: the Poincaré sphere. Write the light as its Stokes vector, a point on a unit sphere:

    \mathbf S = \begin{pmatrix} S_1 \\ S_2 \\ S_3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} |E_x|^2 - |E_y|^2 \\ 2\,\mathrm{Re}(E_x^*E_y) \\ -2\,\mathrm{Im}(E_x^*E_y) \end{pmatrix} = S_0 \begin{pmatrix} \cos 2\chi\,\cos 2\psi \\ \cos 2\chi\,\sin 2\psi \\ \sin 2\chi \end{pmatrix}

    Linear light lies on the equator at longitude 2ψ, right-circular at the north pole, left-circular at the south pole. A wave plate turns the sphere by Γ about the axis through its fast-axis point â = (cos 2θ, sin 2θ, 0), clockwise seen from outside at â:

    \mathbf S' = \mathbf S\cos\Gamma - (\hat{\mathbf a}\times\mathbf S)\sin\Gamma + \hat{\mathbf a}\,(\hat{\mathbf a}\cdot\mathbf S)(1 - \cos\Gamma)

    A half-wave plate is a half turn, a quarter-wave plate a quarter turn. Light whose point lies on the axis (linear along the fast or slow axis) does not move.

  10. Between a polarizer at α and an analyzer at β. Project the output onto the analyzer:

    \frac{I}{I_0} = \cos^2(\beta - \alpha) - \sin 2(\theta - \alpha)\;\sin 2(\theta - \beta)\;\sin^2\frac{\Gamma}{2}
    \begin{aligned} \text{crossed } (\beta = \alpha + 90^\circ):\quad \frac{I}{I_0} &= \sin^2 2(\theta - \alpha)\;\sin^2\frac{\Gamma}{2} \\[5pt] \text{parallel } (\beta = \alpha):\quad \frac{I}{I_0} &= 1 - \sin^2 2(\theta - \alpha)\;\sin^2\frac{\Gamma}{2} \end{aligned}

Recipes

  • Turn linear polarization by β: a half-wave plate with its fast axis at β/2 from the light's polarization. To set a probe at the magic angle, 54.7° from the pump, put the plate at 27.4°.
  • Make circular light: a quarter-wave plate with its fast axis at 45° to the linear polarization; −45° gives the other hand.
  • Find a plate's axes: between crossed polarizers, turn the plate until the light goes out. An axis then lies along the polarizer. The fast one is not marked by this test.
  • Adjust power: a half-wave plate before a polarizer passes cos² 2θ of the light, θ measured from the polarizer's axis.
  • Optical isolator: a polarizer, then a quarter-wave plate at 45°. Light reflected back crosses the plate twice, a half-wave plate at 45°, and returns turned by 90°, so the polarizer blocks it.
  • Two quarter-wave plates with parallel fast axes make a half-wave plate; with crossed fast axes they cancel.

6. A wave plate between polarizers; interference colors in white light

Put the plate between a polarizer at α and an analyzer at β and turn it. Between crossed polarizers light gets through only where the plate changes its polarization: nothing when an axis of the plate lies along the polarizer, the most with the plate at 45°, where I/I₀ = sin²(Γ/2). This is how the axes of an unmarked plate are found, and how a half-wave plate and a polarizer make a variable attenuator.

Face-on: polarizer (black), plate axes (blue fast, red slow), analyzer (gray dashed), the light after the plate (black ellipse) and the part the analyzer passes (orange).

White light: interference colors

In white light the retardance differs for every color, Γ = 2π|Δn|d/λ, so between crossed polarizers some colors are blocked and the plate looks colored. With the plate at 45° the transmission is sin²(πR/λ), and the color depends only on the retardation R = |Δn|d: gray and white below 300 nm, then yellow, orange, the magenta "first-order red" near 550 nm, blue, green, and paler and paler pinks and greens in higher orders. Mineralogists identify crystals in thin sections this way (the Michel-Lévy chart); cellophane or a plastic ruler between polarizers shows the same colors. Parallel polarizers show the complementary colors.

7. Real wave plates: thickness, order and wavelength

A zero-order quartz quarter-wave plate for 633 nm is only 17.5 µm thick, too thin to handle on its own, so real plates come in a few kinds. A multi-order plate is thicker by whole waves, Γ = 2π(m + ¼): sturdy and cheap, but its retardance changes quickly with wavelength, temperature and angle. A compound zero-order plate is two multi-order plates with crossed axes whose thicknesses differ by the zero-order thickness: it behaves like the thin plate. An achromatic plate crosses two materials with different dispersion of Δn (here quartz and MgF₂) so the retardance is right at two wavelengths and close to right between them. Every plate is a quarter- or half-wave plate at one wavelength only; elsewhere it is something else.

8. Young's two slits

A plane wave falls on two narrow slits a distance d apart. Each slit sends out a cylindrical wave, and where the two overlap their fields add. At angle θ the path from the far slit is longer by Δr = d sin θ: bright fringes where Δr = mλ, dark ones where Δr = (m + ½)λ. On a screen at distance L ≫ d the bright fringes sit at ym = mλL/d, Δy = λL/d apart, and I = 4I₁cos²(πdy/λL): four times one slit's intensity at the bright fringes, zero at the dark ones, 2I₁ on average, so energy is conserved. Real slits have a width a, and each slit's own diffraction pattern, sinc²(πay/λL), sets the envelope. More slits keep the bright fringes where they are and make them sharper.

9. Coherence and white-light fringes

Real light is not one endless sine wave. An atom sends out a train of waves, and the phase jumps at random from one train to the next. Fringes need the two waves that meet at a point to belong to the same train, so they fade once the path difference passes the coherence length, ℓc ≈ λ²/Δλ. A laser line (Δλ ≪ 1 nm) gives fringes over meters of path difference, an LED (Δλ ≈ 20 nm) a few tens of fringes, white light only the central few. White fringes are also colored: each color has its own spacing λL/d, and the colors only line up at m = 0.

10. Polarization and interference

Fields add as vectors, so only the parts of two waves with the same polarization interfere: I = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂) Re(ê₁*·ê₂ eiδ). Two beams polarized at right angles make no fringes at all (one of the Fresnel–Arago laws). Light two slits with horizontally polarized light and cover slit 2 with a half-wave plate at θ: that slit's light turns to 2θ and the fringe visibility falls to |cos 2θ|. At θ = 45° the fringes vanish, though each slit still sends the same light to the screen. This ties the two halves of this page together.

Equations

Birefringent crystals

  1. Uniaxial crystal: o- and e-wave

    \begin{aligned} \Delta n &= n_e - n_o \\[5pt] \frac{1}{n^2(\theta)} &= \frac{\cos^2\theta}{n_o^2} + \frac{\sin^2\theta}{n_e^2} \end{aligned}

    θ is the angle between the e-wave's k and the optic axis. The o-wave sees no in every direction.

  2. Walk-off of the e-ray

    \begin{aligned} \tan\theta_\text{ray} &= \frac{n_o^2}{n_e^2}\,\tan\theta \\[5pt] \tan\rho &= \frac{(n_o^2 - n_e^2)\sin\theta\cos\theta}{n_e^2\cos^2\theta + n_o^2\sin^2\theta} \\[5pt] \tan\rho_\text{max} &= \frac{n_o^2 - n_e^2}{2n_on_e} \quad\text{at}\quad \tan\theta = \frac{n_e}{n_o} \end{aligned}

    ρ > 0: the ray lies farther from the optic axis than k (negative crystals such as calcite).

  3. Refraction into a uniaxial crystal

    \begin{aligned} k_x &= n_1 k_0\sin\theta_I \\[5pt] \text{o-wave:}\quad n_1\sin\theta_I &= n_o\sin\theta_o \\[5pt] \text{e-wave:}\quad \frac{k_\perp^2}{n_e^2} + \frac{k_\parallel^2}{n_o^2} &= k_0^2 \end{aligned}

    k∥ and k⊥ are the parts of k along and across the optic axis. With the optic axis perpendicular to the plane of incidence, k∥ = 0 and n1 sin θI = ne sin θe.

  4. Wollaston prism (normal incidence, wedge angle α)

    \begin{aligned} n_o\sin\alpha &= n_e\sin\theta_A, & \sin\varphi_A &= n_e\sin(\theta_A - \alpha) \\[5pt] n_e\sin\alpha &= n_o\sin\theta_B, & \sin\varphi_B &= n_o\sin(\theta_B - \alpha) \\[5pt] \varphi_A - \varphi_B &\approx 2\arcsin\!\big(|\Delta n|\tan\alpha\big) \end{aligned}

Wave plates: Jones calculus

  1. Retardance and thickness

    \begin{aligned} \Gamma &= \frac{2\pi\,|\Delta n|\,d}{\lambda_0} \\[5pt] d_{\lambda/4} &= \left(m + \tfrac14\right)\frac{\lambda_0}{|\Delta n|} \\[5pt] d_{\lambda/2} &= \left(m + \tfrac12\right)\frac{\lambda_0}{|\Delta n|} \\[5pt] \Delta t &= \frac{\Gamma}{\omega} = \frac{|\Delta n|\,d}{c} \end{aligned}

  2. Jones vectors

    \begin{aligned} \text{linear at } \alpha:&\quad \begin{pmatrix} \cos\alpha \\ \sin\alpha \end{pmatrix} \\[6pt] \text{right, left:}&\quad \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{pmatrix},\ \ \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{pmatrix} \\[6pt] \text{elliptical:}&\quad \mathbf R(\psi)\begin{pmatrix} \cos\chi \\ -i\sin\chi \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}

    ψ is the azimuth of the major axis and tan |χ| = b/a; χ > 0 is right-handed (clockwise looking back at the source).

  3. Wave plate with its fast axis at θ

    \begin{aligned} \mathbf J(\theta,\Gamma) &= \mathbf R(\theta)\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\Gamma} \end{pmatrix}\mathbf R(-\theta) \\[6pt] &= e^{i\Gamma/2}\left[\cos\frac{\Gamma}{2}\,\mathbf 1 - i\sin\frac{\Gamma}{2}\,\mathbf M(\theta)\right] \\[6pt] \mathbf R(\theta) &= \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix} \\[6pt] \mathbf M(\theta) &= \begin{pmatrix} \cos 2\theta & \sin 2\theta \\ \sin 2\theta & -\cos 2\theta \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}

  4. Half-wave and quarter-wave plates

    \begin{aligned} \mathbf J_{\lambda/2}(\theta) &= \mathbf M(\theta) \\[6pt] \mathbf J_{\lambda/4}(\theta) &= \frac{e^{i\pi/4}}{\sqrt2}\big[\mathbf 1 - i\,\mathbf M(\theta)\big] \end{aligned}

  5. What they do to linear light at α (φ = α − θ)

    \begin{aligned} \lambda/2:&\quad \text{linear at } 2\theta - \alpha \\[5pt] \lambda/4:&\quad \begin{pmatrix} E_f \\ E_s \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi \\ i\sin\varphi \end{pmatrix}, \quad \frac{b}{a} = |\tan\varphi|^{\pm 1},\quad \chi = -\varphi \end{aligned}

    For a quarter-wave plate χ = −φ when |φ| ≤ 45°. For larger |φ| the major axis lies along the slow axis and sin 2χ = −sin 2φ still holds: φ = 60° gives χ = −30°.

  6. Any plate, linear light in

    \begin{aligned} \tan 2(\psi - \theta) &= \cos\Gamma\;\tan 2(\alpha - \theta) \\[5pt] \sin 2\chi &= -\sin\Gamma\;\sin 2(\alpha - \theta) \end{aligned}

  7. Polarizer

    \mathbf P(\beta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos^2\beta & \sin\beta\cos\beta \\ \sin\beta\cos\beta & \sin^2\beta \end{pmatrix}

Wave plates: Stokes vectors and the Poincaré sphere

  1. Stokes parameters

    \begin{aligned} S_0 &= |E_x|^2 + |E_y|^2 \\[4pt] S_1 &= |E_x|^2 - |E_y|^2 = S_0\cos 2\chi\cos 2\psi \\[4pt] S_2 &= 2\,\mathrm{Re}(E_x^*E_y) = S_0\cos 2\chi\sin 2\psi \\[4pt] S_3 &= -2\,\mathrm{Im}(E_x^*E_y) = S_0\sin 2\chi \end{aligned}
    \tan 2\psi = \frac{S_2}{S_1}, \qquad \sin 2\chi = \frac{S_3}{S_0}

  2. A wave plate turns the Poincaré sphere

    \begin{aligned} \mathbf S' &= \mathbf S\cos\Gamma - (\hat{\mathbf a}\times\mathbf S)\sin\Gamma + \hat{\mathbf a}\,(\hat{\mathbf a}\cdot\mathbf S)(1 - \cos\Gamma) \\[5pt] \hat{\mathbf a} &= (\cos 2\theta,\ \sin 2\theta,\ 0) \end{aligned}

  3. Mueller matrix of a wave plate (c = cos 2θ, s = sin 2θ)

    \mathbf M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & c^2 + s^2\cos\Gamma & cs\,(1 - \cos\Gamma) & -s\sin\Gamma \\ 0 & cs\,(1 - \cos\Gamma) & s^2 + c^2\cos\Gamma & c\sin\Gamma \\ 0 & s\sin\Gamma & -c\sin\Gamma & \cos\Gamma \end{pmatrix}

  4. Wave plate between a polarizer at α and an analyzer at β

    \frac{I}{I_0} = \cos^2(\beta - \alpha) - \sin 2(\theta - \alpha)\,\sin 2(\theta - \beta)\,\sin^2\frac{\Gamma}{2}

  5. Interference colors (plate at 45°, retardation R = |Δn|d)

    \begin{aligned} T_\text{crossed}(\lambda) &= \sin^2\frac{\pi R}{\lambda} \\[5pt] T_\text{parallel}(\lambda) &= \cos^2\frac{\pi R}{\lambda} \end{aligned}

    The color is ∫ T(λ) x̄, ȳ, z̄(λ) dλ for a flat (equal-energy) white source, converted to sRGB with white kept white.

  6. Compound and achromatic plates (axes crossed)

    \begin{aligned} \text{compound zero-order:}\quad \Gamma &= \frac{2\pi\,|\Delta n|\,(d_1 - d_2)}{\lambda} \\[5pt] \text{achromatic:}\quad \Gamma(\lambda) &= \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,\big(\Delta n_1(\lambda)\,d_1 - \Delta n_2(\lambda)\,d_2\big) \end{aligned}

    For the achromatic plate d₁ and d₂ solve Γ(λ₁) = Γ(λ₂) = the target: two linear equations.

Interference

  1. Two slits: path difference and fringes

    \begin{aligned} \Delta r &= d\sin\theta \\[5pt] \text{bright:}\quad d\sin\theta_m &= m\lambda, & y_m &= m\,\frac{\lambda L}{d} \\[5pt] \text{dark:}\quad d\sin\theta_m &= \left(m + \tfrac12\right)\lambda, & y_m &= \left(m + \tfrac12\right)\frac{\lambda L}{d} \\[5pt] \Delta y &= y_{m+1} - y_m = \frac{\lambda L}{d} \end{aligned}

    Small angles, L ≫ y: sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ θ = y/L.

  2. Adding the two fields

    \begin{aligned} E &= a\sin(kr_1 - \omega t) + a\sin(kr_2 - \omega t) \\[5pt] &= 2a\cos\frac{\Delta\phi}{2}\;\sin(kr_\text{avg} - \omega t) \\[5pt] \Delta\phi &= k\,\Delta r = \frac{2\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda} \approx \frac{2\pi d}{\lambda L}\,y \\[5pt] I(y) &= 4I_1\cos^2\frac{\pi d\,y}{\lambda L} \end{aligned}

    The average over the fringes is 2I₁, the sum of the two slits' intensities.

  3. Slit width and N slits

    \begin{aligned} I &= I_1\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2\left(\frac{\sin(N\delta/2)}{\sin(\delta/2)}\right)^2 \\[5pt] \beta &= \frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}, \qquad \delta = \frac{2\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda} \end{aligned}

    I₁ is one slit's intensity at the center. Principal maxima N²I₁ at d sin θ = mλ with N − 2 small maxima between; envelope zeros at a sin θ = λ, 2λ, …; with d/a whole, those orders are missing.

  4. Phasors

    \begin{aligned} A\,e^{i\varphi} &= \sum_j A_j\,e^{i\varphi_j} \\[5pt] \text{two equal phasors:}\quad A &= 2A_1\left|\cos\frac{\delta}{2}\right| \end{aligned}

  5. Coherence

    \begin{aligned} \ell_c &= \frac{\lambda^2}{\Delta\lambda} = \frac{c}{\Delta\nu} \\[5pt] \text{Gaussian line:}\quad V(\Delta r) &= \exp\!\left[-\frac{(\pi\,\Delta r/\ell_c)^2}{4\ln 2}\right] \\[5pt] \text{random phase jumps:}\quad V(\Delta r) &= e^{-\Delta r/\ell} \\[5pt] I &= 2I_1\big[1 + V\cos(k\,\Delta r)\big] \end{aligned}

    V = (Imax − Imin)/(Imax + Imin) is the fringe visibility. For a Gaussian line V = ½ at Δr = (2 ln 2/π)ℓc = 0.44ℓc; ℓ is the mean length of the wave trains.

  6. Polarization and interference

    \begin{aligned} I &= I_1 + I_2 + 2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\;\mathrm{Re}\!\left(\hat{\mathbf e}_1^*\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_2\,e^{i\delta}\right) \\[5pt] V &= \frac{2\sqrt{I_1I_2}}{I_1 + I_2}\,\big|\hat{\mathbf e}_1^*\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_2\big| \end{aligned}

    A half-wave plate at θ over one slit: ê₁*·ê₂ = cos 2θ. A quarter-wave plate: cos²θ + i sin²θ.
