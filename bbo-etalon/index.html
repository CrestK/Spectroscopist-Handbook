BBO crystal and etalon for a narrowband pulse

A femtosecond pulse is frequency doubled in a BBO crystal and the second harmonic goes through an etalon, which keeps one narrow band: a picosecond pulse with a sharp rise and an exponential decay, as used for the Raman pump in FSRS. Enter the wavelength, the bandwidth of the fundamental, the bandwidth you want and the etalon. The page works out the second harmonic from each crystal length and the pulse that leaves the etalon, and lists which crystals give a single clean pulse of that bandwidth; the thinnest one that works is the suggestion. Equations are at the bottom.

The fundamental is a Gaussian pulse at 2λ₂, given by its bandwidth, its duration, or both; with both, the extra bandwidth is a linear chirp. The etalon has two equal, lossless mirrors and one transmission peak on the centre of the second harmonic (in the lab: the gap or the tilt tuned onto it). n only sets the listed gap.

What counts as a clean pulse

% % of the wanted % of the decay time

Ripple: how far the tail of the pulse departs from a smooth exponential once the second harmonic has passed. It comes from neighbouring etalon peaks that let light through, which shows as separate round trips, and from sharp edges on the second harmonic, which add a step to the decay every round trip. Rise: a thick crystal stretches the second harmonic, so the pulse no longer switches on fast. Bandwidth: the FWHM of the transmitted band.

Combinations

SH: the second harmonic leaving the crystal. Pulse: what leaves the etalon. Decay: the 1/e time of its intensity, set by the etalon. In one band: the share of the transmitted light within half an FSR of the centre. Etalon passes: the share of the second harmonic's energy that the etalon transmits. Values in red miss a criterion. Click a row to plot that crystal below.

Conversion efficiency is not part of the check: at low intensity a thicker crystal makes more second harmonic, at the intensities usually used with thin crystals the conversion saturates.

The thin-crystal second harmonic is √2 times as wide as the fundamental. A longer crystal narrows it once the group-velocity mismatch over its length passes the pulse duration. Below FSR/2 only one etalon peak gets light. Dots are the crystals in the list, filled when they work.

ripple on the decay rise as % of decay limits

A clean pulse has both curves under their dashed limits. Thin crystals give ripple when the second harmonic covers several etalon peaks; thick ones give a slow rise. Dips in the ripple come where the stretched second harmonic lasts a whole number of round trips.

Methods and equations

  1. BBO and phase matching

    n² = 1 + Σi Bi λ² / (λ² − Ci),  o: B = 0.90291, 0.83155, 0.76536; C = 0.003926, 0.018786, 60.01;  e: B = 1.151075, 0.21803, 0.656; C = 0.007142, 0.02259, 263
    type I, o + o → e:  ne(θ, λ₂) = no(λ₁),  1/ne(θ)² = cos²θ/no² + sin²θ/ne²

    λ in µm. Sellmeier equations of Tamošauskas et al., Opt. Mater. Express 8, 1410 (2018), valid from 0.188 to 5.2 µm. θ comes out at 29.2° for 800 nm, 23.4° for 1030 nm and 20.0° for 1500 nm.

  2. Group-velocity mismatch

    GVM = 1/v2 − 1/v1 = (ng,2 − ng,1) / c,  ng = n − λ dn/dλ,  T = GVM · L

    The second harmonic falls behind the fundamental by GVM per mm: 193 fs/mm at 800 nm, 95 fs/mm at 1030 nm, 53 fs/mm at 1200 nm and only 2.6 fs/mm at 1500 nm. Near 1500 nm the published dispersion fits disagree by about 6 fs/mm, so treat results above λ₂ ≈ 700 nm as rough. T is how long the second harmonic gets.

  3. Second harmonic from a crystal of length L

    ℰ̃2(Ω) ∝ ∫ ℰ̃1(Ω/2 + u) ℰ̃1(Ω/2 − u) · [1 − e−iΔk L] / (iΔk L) du,  Δk = ke(2ω₁ + Ω) − ko(ω₁ + Ω/2 + u) − ko(ω₁ + Ω/2 − u)
    to first order, Δk = GVM · Ω:  |ℰ̃2|² = |ℰ̃2,thin|² · sinc²(GVM · Ω L / 2),  sinc² FWHM = 0.886 / (c · GVM · L)

    Undepleted fundamental, plane waves. A thin crystal gives Δν₂ = √2 Δν₁ and τ₂ = τ₁/√2. In time, the first-order result is the square of the fundamental smeared over T: a flat top with edges as sharp as τ₂. The page uses the full Δk from the Sellmeier equations, which adds the dispersion of both pulses in the crystal; that matters near 1500 nm, where the GVM is close to 0, and for short pulses in long crystals.

  4. Etalon

    t(Ω) = (1 − R) / (1 − R eiΩtrt),  |t|² = 1 / [1 + F sin²(πx/FSR)],  F = 4R/(1 − R)²,  trt = 1/(c · FSR)
    peak FWHM = (2 FSR/π) arcsin(1/√F),  decay (1/e) τc = trt / (2 ln(1/R)),  gap d = 1/(2n · FSR)
    R for a wanted FWHM: F = 1/sin²(π FWHM/(2 FSR)), R = [F + 2 − 2√(F + 1)]/F;  FSR for a wanted FWHM: FSR = π FWHM / (2 arcsin(1/√F))

    x is the wavenumber from the centre of the second harmonic, where one peak sits. The same equations as the etalon page.

  5. The transmitted pulse

    ET(t) = (1 − R) Σj≥0 Rj E2(t − j trt)  ⇔  ℰ̃T(Ω) = t(Ω) ℰ̃2(Ω)

    Computed as ℰ̃2·t on a grid and one FFT to time; the grid spans 24 decay times, so the wrap-around is below 10⁻¹⁰ of the peak. The spectral FWHM of the pulse is found on the exact spectrum by bisection.

  6. What counts as a clean pulse

    ripple = (max − min) / (max + min) of IT(t) et/τc,  from t = T/2 + 2τ₂ until the tail is down to 1 % of the peak
    rise = 10–90 % rise time of IT, as a share of τc;  bandwidth = FWHM of |ℰ̃T|² around the centre

    After the second harmonic has passed, a single etalon band decays exactly as e−t/τc, so IT et/τc is flat and the ripple is 0. For a Gaussian second harmonic the beat with the neighbouring peaks gives about ±4√S, S = exp[−4 ln 2 (FSR/Δν₂)²]: ±18 % at FSR = 1.5 Δν₂ and ±1.6 % at 2 Δν₂. A crystal works when it meets all three limits; the thinnest that works is suggested, because it keeps the second harmonic short and Gaussian, so the pulse rises fastest. Not included: depletion of the fundamental and the conversion efficiency, focusing, spatial walk-off and the angular acceptance of the crystal, mirror loss, coating dispersion, a peak away from the centre of the second harmonic, and absorption. The fundamental is a Gaussian with at most a linear chirp.
