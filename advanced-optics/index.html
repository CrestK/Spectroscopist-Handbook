1. Waves, pulses and phasors

A wave is a disturbance that travels: any shape f(z − vt) moves along +z unchanged, and f(z + vt) along −z. For a harmonic wave the source sets the period T, the medium sets the speed v = c/n, and together they fix the wavelength λ = vT and the wavenumber k = 2π/λ. Press Play and compare the snapshot (the whole wave at one time), the history graph (one point over time) and the phasor (the turning complex number whose real part is the wave).

The wave number k and the wavelength λ (right) follow from both: kv = ω and vT = λ. Click a quantity to edit it in the converter; after an edit the shaded boxes are the quantities that changed.

Examples:

Going from one quantity straight to the others. v is the phase velocity: c in vacuum, c/n in a medium. λ₀ is the vacuum wavelength and ν̃ = 1/λ₀ the wavenumber used in spectroscopy, which, like f, does not change in a medium.

From the wavelength λ

\begin{aligned} f &= \frac{v}{\lambda} \\[5pt] \omega &= \frac{2\pi v}{\lambda} \\[5pt] k &= \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \\[5pt] T &= \frac{\lambda}{v} \end{aligned}

From the frequency f

\begin{aligned} \lambda &= \frac{v}{f} \\[5pt] \omega &= 2\pi f \\[5pt] k &= \frac{2\pi f}{v} \\[5pt] T &= \frac{1}{f} \end{aligned}

From the angular frequency ω

\begin{aligned} \lambda &= \frac{2\pi v}{\omega} \\[5pt] f &= \frac{\omega}{2\pi} \\[5pt] k &= \frac{\omega}{v} \\[5pt] T &= \frac{2\pi}{\omega} \end{aligned}

From the wave number k

\begin{aligned} \lambda &= \frac{2\pi}{k} \\[5pt] f &= \frac{kv}{2\pi} \\[5pt] \omega &= kv \\[5pt] T &= \frac{2\pi}{kv} \end{aligned}

From the wavenumber ν̃ (light)

\begin{aligned} \lambda_0 &= \frac{1}{\tilde\nu} \\[5pt] \lambda &= \frac{1}{n\,\tilde\nu} \\[5pt] f &= c\,\tilde\nu \\[5pt] \omega &= 2\pi c\,\tilde\nu \\[5pt] k &= 2\pi n\,\tilde\nu \end{aligned}

Handy numbers for light

\begin{aligned} \tilde\nu\ [\text{cm}^{-1}] &= \frac{10^{7}}{\lambda_0\ [\text{nm}]} \\[5pt] f\ [\text{THz}] &= 0.029979\;\tilde\nu\ [\text{cm}^{-1}] \\[5pt] E\ [\text{eV}] &= \frac{1239.84}{\lambda_0\ [\text{nm}]} \\[5pt] E\ [\text{eV}] &= \frac{\tilde\nu\ [\text{cm}^{-1}]}{8065.54} \end{aligned}

2. Light in vacuum: E, B, intensity and polarization

Maxwell's equations in free space give waves with k² = μ₀ε₀ω², so every frequency travels at c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀). E, B and k are mutually perpendicular and right-handed, E and B are in phase, and B₀ = E₀/c. The direction of E is the polarization: it can stay in one plane (linear) or turn (circular, elliptical). Detectors respond to the intensity I = ½cε₀E₀², the power per area averaged over a period.

3. Refractive index of real glasses

In a dielectric the wave moves at v = c/n with n = √εr. The frequency stays the one the source set; the wavelength shrinks to λ₀/n. n depends on wavelength because bound electrons and ions respond like Lorentz oscillators: glass has resonances in the ultraviolet and the infrared, and visible light sits between them, where n rises toward the blue (normal dispersion). The curves are measured Sellmeier fits for real materials (sources in the notes); the glass with n = 1.57 used in part 7 lies between N-BK7 and N-F2.

Show:

n(λ) of each material over the range its formula was measured for; the colored strip marks the visible. All curves fall with wavelength between the UV and IR resonances. Hover to read values and click to put a wavelength in the table; the dashed line is the wavelength in the table.

nd is n at 587.6 nm. The Abbe number Vd = (nd − 1)/(nF − nC) is small for glasses that spread colors widely (flints) and large for low-dispersion ones (crowns, CaF₂). ng and GVD are used in part 4.

Cauchy's formula and the oscillators behind it

Far below a single resonance the Lorentz model gives Cauchy's formula n ≈ 1 + A(1 + B/λ²). Fit it to a material over 400–700 nm and see where it holds. Each Sellmeier term Bλ²/(λ² − λj²) is an undamped Lorentz oscillator at λj, so the fit coefficients are real resonances.

White light through a prism

Each wavelength refracts by a different angle because n(λ) differs: the prism turns dispersion into a spread of colors. Change the glass and the angle of incidence; "Minimum deviation" sets the symmetric path for 587.6 nm.

4. Pulses in glass: phase velocity, group velocity and dispersion

A short pulse is a sum of many frequencies. In glass its crests move at the phase velocity c/n, its envelope at the group velocity c/ng with ng = n − λ dn/dλ, and because ng itself changes with wavelength (group-velocity dispersion, GVD) the pulse spreads out. Part A follows a pulse through a thin slab at the scale of the wavelength; part B follows a femtosecond pulse through centimeters of glass.

A. A pulse meets a glass slab

The field Re E (black) and its envelope (grey) along z; the glass is shaded. The orange dashed envelope is the same pulse with no glass. At the front face about 4 % of the power reflects with its field flipped (r < 0, a π phase shift); inside the wavelength is λ₀/n, the field is smaller (t = 2/(1 + n)) and the pulse falls behind the orange one; the back face sends a second, unflipped reflection. Later reflections are left out.

In normal dispersion red light is faster than blue, so the red end leads, the blue end trails and the pulse is stretched and chirped; its spectrum is unchanged.

5. Conductors and skin depth

Free charges add a current J = σE, and the wave equation then has a complex wavenumber k̃ = k + iκ: the field decays as e−κz over the skin depth d = 1/κ. In a good conductor (σ ≫ εω) d ≈ √(2/σμω), the wavelength inside is only 2πd, and B lags E by 45°. Free charge inside relaxes in τ = ε/σ (1.5 × 10⁻¹⁹ s in copper), so a conductor stays neutral.

Examples:

Skin depth against frequency for the material's DC conductivity. Where σ falls below εω (the dotted line) the material behaves like a lossy dielectric and d levels off. Above about 10¹³ Hz the DC conductivity of a metal no longer applies (the electrons cannot follow the field), so the grey band is a model only; see real metals below.

6. Absorption and anomalous dispersion: the Lorentz oscillator

A bound electron driven by the light field is a damped oscillator. Near its resonance ω₀ the medium absorbs, and n changes fast: below ω₀ it rises with frequency (normal dispersion), within γ/2 of ω₀ it falls (anomalous dispersion), and above it rises again. n is largest and smallest exactly where α is half its peak, so the anomalous region is as wide as the absorption line. The first example puts numbers on a 589 nm line.

7. Reflection and refraction: s and p, R and T, Brewster's angle

At an interface the tangential E and H and the normal D and B must match. That fixes the directions (law of reflection, Snell's law) and the amplitudes (Fresnel equations), and the amplitudes depend on how E is oriented relative to the plane of incidence. The two cases, s and p, behave differently, and every other polarization is a mix of them.

Plane of incidence
The plane that holds the incident ray and the surface normal: the x–z plane here, the plane of the page.
s polarization (blue)
E perpendicular to the plane of incidence (s from German senkrecht; also called TE). Here E points along y, out of or into the page: drawn as ⊙ (out) and ⊗ (in).
p polarization (red)
E parallel to the plane of incidence (also TM). E lies in the page, at right angles to each ray: drawn as arrows.
Intensity I
Power per area, averaged over a period, in a medium of index n. Detectors and eyes measure I, not E.
I = \tfrac12\,n\,c\,\varepsilon_0 E_0^2
Reflectance R
The fraction of the incident power that is reflected.
R = \frac{I_R}{I_I} = \left|\frac{E_{0R}}{E_{0I}}\right|^2 = |r|^2
Transmittance T
The fraction that crosses into medium 2. The n₂/n₁ is there because I ∝ n, the cosines because the beam gets wider or narrower when it bends. With no absorption R + T = 1.
T = \frac{n_2\cos\theta_T}{n_1\cos\theta_I}\,|t|^2 = \alpha\beta\,|t|^2
Brewster's angle θB
There Rp = 0, so reflected light is purely s. The reflected and refracted rays are 90° apart: the electrons in medium 2 oscillate along ET, which then points along the reflected direction, and a dipole does not radiate along its own axis.
\tan\theta_B = \frac{n_2}{n_1}
Total internal reflection
From high to low index beyond the critical angle θc, Rs = Rp = 1 and the field in medium 2 decays within about a wavelength (an evanescent wave).
\sin\theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1}
Phase on reflection
From low to high index r < 0 at normal incidence and the reflected field is flipped (a π phase shift). This page uses Griffiths' sign for p (the field directions in the diagrams below), which gives rp = rs at normal incidence; Hecht's textbook flips the sign of rp.
r = \frac{n_1 - n_2}{n_1 + n_2} \qquad (\theta_I = 0)

8. Polarization through a real optic: a glass window at an angle

Because s and p reflect differently, any tilted optic changes the polarization of the light that passes it. A window at Brewster's angle passes p with no reflection loss but reflects about 15 % of s at each surface, which is why a gas laser with Brewster windows on its tube lases in p. A stack of plates removes more s at every surface (a pile-of-plates polarizer). And glare off water or glass is mostly s, horizontal, which polarized sunglasses block with a vertical transmission axis.

9. Polarizers and Malus's law

An ideal linear polarizer passes the component of E along its transmission axis, so the intensity drops by cos²θ (Malus's law), with θ the angle between the light's polarization and the axis. Unpolarized (natural) light loses half its intensity at the first polarizer, whatever the angle. Set up to three polarizers, or let the middle one turn. Angles are measured from vertical.

Equations

Waves

  1. Wave equation and its solutions

    \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2} = \frac{1}{v^2}\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial t^2}
    \begin{aligned} f(z,t) &= g(z - vt) &&\text{moves along } {+z} \\ f(z,t) &= g(z + vt) &&\text{moves along } {-z} \end{aligned}

  2. Harmonic wave

    D(z,t) = A\cos(kz \mp \omega t + \varphi_0)
    \begin{aligned} \omega &= 2\pi f = \frac{2\pi}{T} \\[5pt] k &= \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \\[5pt] v &= \frac{\omega}{k} = f\lambda = \frac{\lambda}{T} \end{aligned}

    The source sets ω; the medium sets v = c/n; both together set k and λ. A > 0; φ₀ is the initial phase.

  3. Complex notation and phasors

    \begin{aligned} D &= \operatorname{Re}\!\left[\tilde A\, e^{i(kz \mp \omega t)}\right] \\ \tilde A &= A\,e^{i\varphi_0} \end{aligned}
    \begin{aligned} A\,e^{i\varphi} &= A_1 e^{i\varphi_1} + A_2 e^{i\varphi_2} \\ A^2 &= A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2A_1A_2\cos(\varphi_1 - \varphi_2) \end{aligned}

    At fixed z the phasor turns clockwise for a wave along +z (−ωt) and counterclockwise along −z. Waves of one frequency add like vectors.

  4. Three dimensions

    \begin{aligned} \text{plane:}\quad D &= A\cos(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r - \omega t + \varphi_0) \\[5pt] |\mathbf k| &= \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \\[5pt] \text{spherical:}\quad D &= \frac{A}{r}\cos(kr - \omega t) \\[5pt] \text{cylindrical:}\quad D &= \frac{A}{\sqrt r}\cos(kr - \omega t) \end{aligned}

Light in vacuum

  1. Dispersion relation

    \begin{aligned} k^2 &= \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\omega^2 \\[5pt] c &= \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} = 2.99792458\times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s} \end{aligned}

  2. Plane wave: transverse, E and B in phase

    \begin{aligned} \mathbf E &= E_0\,\hat{\mathbf n}\,e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r - \omega t)} \\[5pt] \mathbf B &= \frac{1}{c}\,\hat{\mathbf k}\times\mathbf E \\[5pt] B_0 &= \frac{E_0}{c} \end{aligned}

  3. Polarization

    \mathbf E = \hat{\mathbf x}\,E_{0x}\cos(kz - \omega t) + \hat{\mathbf y}\,E_{0y}\cos(kz - \omega t + \delta)

    δ = 0 or π: linear. E₀ₓ = E₀ᵧ and δ = ±90°: circular. Anything else: elliptical. With δ = +90° E turns counterclockwise seen facing the oncoming light (left-circular in Hecht's convention).

  4. Energy and intensity

    \begin{aligned} u &= \tfrac12\left(\varepsilon_0 E^2 + \frac{B^2}{\mu_0}\right) = \varepsilon_0 E^2 \\[5pt] \mathbf S &= \frac{1}{\mu_0}\,\mathbf E\times\mathbf B = c\,u\,\hat{\mathbf k} \\[5pt] \langle u\rangle &= \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_0 E_0^2 \\[5pt] I &= \langle S\rangle = \tfrac12\,c\,\varepsilon_0 E_0^2 \end{aligned}

    In a medium of index n, I = ½ncε₀E₀². Momentum density ⟨u⟩/c; radiation pressure I/c when absorbed and 2I/c when reflected.

  5. Sources

    \begin{aligned} \text{Gaussian beam:}\quad I_\text{peak} &= \frac{2P}{\pi w^2} \\[5pt] \text{Gaussian pulse:}\quad P_\text{peak} &= 2\sqrt{\frac{\ln 2}{\pi}}\;\frac{E_p}{\tau} \approx 0.939\,\frac{E_p}{\tau} \\[5pt] \text{point source:}\quad I &= \frac{P}{4\pi r^2} \\[5pt] \text{photon flux:}\quad \Phi &= \frac{I\lambda}{hc} \end{aligned}

    w is the 1/e² radius of the beam and τ the FWHM of the pulse.

Light in dielectrics

  1. Linear dielectric

    \begin{aligned} \mathbf P &= \varepsilon_0\chi_e\,\mathbf E \\[5pt] \mathbf D &= \varepsilon_0(1 + \chi_e)\,\mathbf E = \varepsilon\,\mathbf E \\[5pt] v &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}} \\[5pt] n &= \frac{c}{v} = \sqrt{\varepsilon_r\mu_r} \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_r} \\[5pt] \lambda &= \frac{\lambda_0}{n} \end{aligned}

  2. Sellmeier equation, an undamped sum of Lorentz oscillators

    \begin{aligned} n^2 &= 1 + \sum_j \frac{B_j\,\lambda^2}{\lambda^2 - \lambda_j^2} \\[5pt] B_j &= \frac{N_j f_j q^2}{\varepsilon_0 m\,\omega_j^2} \\[5pt] \omega_j &= \frac{2\pi c}{\lambda_j} \end{aligned}

  3. Cauchy's formula (far below one resonance)

    \begin{aligned} n &\approx 1 + A\left(1 + \frac{B}{\lambda^2}\right) \\[5pt] A &= \frac{Nq^2}{2\varepsilon_0 m\,\omega_0^2} \\[5pt] B &= \left(\frac{2\pi c}{\omega_0}\right)^2 = \lambda_0^2 \end{aligned}

    The page fits n = A′ + B′/λ² and converts: A = A′ − 1, B = B′/A.

  4. Abbe number

    V_d = \frac{n_d - 1}{n_F - n_C}

    d = 587.56 nm, F = 486.13 nm, C = 656.27 nm.

  5. Prism

    \begin{aligned} \sin\theta_1 &= n\sin\theta_2 \\[5pt] \theta_3 &= A - \theta_2 \\[5pt] n\sin\theta_3 &= \sin\theta_4 \\[5pt] \delta &= \theta_1 + \theta_4 - A \\[5pt] n &= \frac{\sin\big((A + \delta_\text{min})/2\big)}{\sin(A/2)} \end{aligned}

    A is the apex angle and δ the deviation; the last line holds at minimum deviation.

Pulses

  1. Phase and group velocity, group-velocity dispersion

    \begin{aligned} v_p &= \frac{c}{n} \\[5pt] v_g &= \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{c}{n_g} \\[5pt] n_g &= n - \lambda\,\frac{dn}{d\lambda} \\[5pt] \beta_2 &= \frac{d^2k}{d\omega^2} = \frac{\lambda^3}{2\pi c^2}\,\frac{d^2n}{d\lambda^2} \\[5pt] \varphi_2 &= \beta_2 L \end{aligned}

    φ₂ is the group-delay dispersion (GDD) of a thickness L.

  2. Gaussian pulse after a GDD φ₂

    E(t) \propto \sqrt{\frac{T_0^2}{T_0^2 - i\varphi_2}}\;\exp\!\left[-\frac{t^2}{2\,(T_0^2 - i\varphi_2)}\right] e^{-i\omega_0 t}
    \begin{aligned} T_0 &= \frac{\tau_0}{2\sqrt{\ln 2}} \\[5pt] \tau &= \tau_0\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{4\ln 2\;\varphi_2}{\tau_0^2}\right)^2} \\[5pt] \omega(t) &= \omega_0 + \frac{t\,\varphi_2}{T_0^4 + \varphi_2^2} \end{aligned}

    For φ₂ > 0 (normal dispersion) the frequency rises from the front of the pulse to the back: red leads.

  3. Normal incidence

    \begin{aligned} r &= \frac{n_1 - n_2}{n_1 + n_2} \\[5pt] t &= \frac{2n_1}{n_1 + n_2} \\[5pt] R &= r^2 \\[5pt] T &= \frac{n_2}{n_1}\,t^2 = 1 - R \end{aligned}

Conductors

  1. Ohm's law and free-charge relaxation

    \begin{aligned} \mathbf J &= \sigma\,\mathbf E \\[5pt] \rho_f(t) &= \rho_f(0)\,e^{-t/\tau} \\[5pt] \tau &= \frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma} \end{aligned}

  2. Complex wavenumber

    \begin{aligned} \nabla^2\mathbf E &= \mu\varepsilon\,\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2} + \mu\sigma\,\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t} \\[5pt] \tilde k^2 &= \mu\varepsilon\,\omega^2 + i\mu\sigma\omega \\[5pt] \tilde k &= k + i\kappa \end{aligned}

  3. Real and imaginary parts, skin depth

    \begin{aligned} k &= \omega\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon\mu}{2}}\left[\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon\omega}\right)^2} + 1\right]^{1/2} \\[5pt] \kappa &= \omega\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon\mu}{2}}\left[\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon\omega}\right)^2} - 1\right]^{1/2} \\[5pt] d &= \frac{1}{\kappa} \end{aligned}
    d \approx \sqrt{\frac{2}{\sigma\mu\omega}} \qquad (\sigma \gg \varepsilon\omega)

  4. Fields in a conductor

    \begin{aligned} \mathbf E &= E_0\,e^{-\kappa z}\cos(kz - \omega t + \delta_E)\,\hat{\mathbf x} \\[5pt] \mathbf B &= \frac{K}{\omega}\,E_0\,e^{-\kappa z}\cos(kz - \omega t + \delta_E + \phi)\,\hat{\mathbf y} \\[5pt] K &= |\tilde k| = \sqrt{k^2 + \kappa^2} \\[5pt] \phi &= \arctan\frac{\kappa}{k} \end{aligned}

    φ → 45° for a good conductor. The magnetic energy is √(1 + (σ/εω)²) times the electric energy.

  5. Measured optical constants

    \begin{aligned} \tilde n &= n + i\kappa \\[5pt] E &\propto e^{-2\pi\kappa z/\lambda} \\[5pt] d &= \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\kappa} \\[5pt] R &= \left|\frac{\tilde n - 1}{\tilde n + 1}\right|^2 \end{aligned}

    Here κ is the dimensionless extinction coefficient; R is the reflectance at normal incidence.

Lorentz oscillator

  1. Driven, damped electron

    \ddot x + \gamma\,\dot x + \omega_0^2\,x = \frac{q}{m}\,E_0\,e^{-i\omega t}
    \tilde x = \frac{(q/m)\,E_0}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega}

    The dipole lags the field by arctan[γω/(ω₀² − ω²)]: 0 below resonance, 90° at it, 180° far above.

  2. Susceptibility and complex index

    \begin{aligned} \tilde\chi &= \frac{Nq^2}{\varepsilon_0 m}\sum_j \frac{f_j}{\omega_j^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma_j\omega} \\[5pt] \tilde n &= \sqrt{1 + \tilde\chi} \\[5pt] \tilde k &= \frac{\tilde n\,\omega}{c} = k + i\kappa \\[5pt] \alpha &= 2\kappa \\[5pt] n &= \frac{ck}{\omega} \end{aligned}

  3. Near one resonance (ω + ω₀ ≈ 2ω₀, dilute)

    \begin{aligned} \alpha &= \frac{Nq^2 f}{2\varepsilon_0 m c}\;\frac{\gamma/2}{(\omega - \omega_0)^2 + (\gamma/2)^2} \\[5pt] n - 1 &= \frac{Nq^2 f}{4\varepsilon_0 m\,\omega_0}\;\frac{\omega_0 - \omega}{(\omega - \omega_0)^2 + (\gamma/2)^2} \end{aligned}

  4. Peak absorption and the extremes of n

    \begin{aligned} \alpha_\text{max} &= \frac{Nq^2 f}{\varepsilon_0 m c\,\gamma} \\[5pt] \omega(n_\text{max}) &= \omega_0 - \frac{\gamma}{2} \\[5pt] \omega(n_\text{min}) &= \omega_0 + \frac{\gamma}{2} \\[5pt] \alpha\!\left(\omega_0 \pm \tfrac{\gamma}{2}\right) &= \frac{\alpha_\text{max}}{2} \\[5pt] (n - 1)_\text{max} &= \frac{Nq^2 f}{4\varepsilon_0 m\,\omega_0\gamma} \end{aligned}

    The anomalous region is γ wide in ω, so the FWHM Δν wide in frequency; γ = 2πΔν.

Interfaces

  1. Phase matching: law of reflection and Snell's law

    \begin{aligned} k_I\sin\theta_I &= k_R\sin\theta_R = k_T\sin\theta_T \\ \theta_R &= \theta_I \\ n_1\sin\theta_I &= n_2\sin\theta_T \end{aligned}

  2. Wave vectors

    \begin{aligned} \mathbf k_I &= n_1 k_0\,(\sin\theta_I,\ 0,\ \cos\theta_I) \\[5pt] \mathbf k_R &= n_1 k_0\,(\sin\theta_I,\ 0,\ -\cos\theta_I) \\[5pt] \mathbf k_T &= n_2 k_0\,(\sin\theta_T,\ 0,\ \cos\theta_T) \\[5pt] k_0 &= \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0} = \frac{\omega}{c} \end{aligned}

  3. Fresnel equations in Griffiths' notation (μ₁ = μ₂)

    \begin{aligned} \alpha &= \frac{\cos\theta_T}{\cos\theta_I} \\[5pt] \beta &= \frac{n_2}{n_1} \end{aligned}
    \begin{aligned} r_s &= \frac{1 - \alpha\beta}{1 + \alpha\beta} & \qquad t_s &= \frac{2}{1 + \alpha\beta} \\[6pt] r_p &= \frac{\alpha - \beta}{\alpha + \beta} & \qquad t_p &= \frac{2}{\alpha + \beta} \end{aligned}

    p field directions: EI ∥ (cos θI, 0, −sin θI), ER ∥ (cos θI, 0, sin θI), ET ∥ (cos θT, 0, −sin θT); s: all along +ŷ. At 45° rp = −rs², so Rp = Rs² there (1.18 % and 10.85 % for n = 1.57). For an absorbing medium 2 or total internal reflection the same formulas hold with complex ñ₂ and cos θT.

  4. Reflectance and transmittance

    \begin{aligned} R &= |r|^2 \\ T &= \alpha\beta\,|t|^2 \\ R + T &= 1 \end{aligned}

    In general T = Re(ñ₂ cos θT)/(n₁ cos θI) |t|², with ñ₂* for p.

  5. Brewster's angle

    \tan\theta_B = \frac{n_2}{n_1}

    At θB, rp = 0 and θB + θT = 90°: the reflected light is purely s. Fresnel's sine and tangent forms show why:

    \begin{aligned} r_s &= -\,\frac{\sin(\theta_I - \theta_T)}{\sin(\theta_I + \theta_T)} \\[6pt] r_p &= \frac{\tan(\theta_I - \theta_T)}{\tan(\theta_I + \theta_T)} \end{aligned}

    tan(θI + θT) grows without limit as θI + θT → 90°, so rp = 0 there and changes sign beyond; the sine never does, so rs is never zero. (This rp has Hecht's sign; Griffiths' is its negative.)

  6. Critical angle and the evanescent wave

    \sin\theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1} \qquad (n_1 > n_2)
    \begin{aligned} E_T &\propto e^{-z/\delta} \\[5pt] \delta &= \frac{\lambda_0}{2\pi\sqrt{n_1^2\sin^2\theta_I - n_2^2}} \end{aligned}

    Beyond θc, Rs = Rp = 1 and the field in medium 2 decays within about a wavelength.

Polarization in optics

  1. Plates (incoherent multiple reflections, no absorption)

    \begin{aligned} T_\text{one plate} &= \frac{1 - R}{1 + R} \\[5pt] T_{N\ \text{plates}} &= \frac{1 - R}{1 + (2N - 1)R} \\[5pt] P &= \frac{T_p - T_s}{T_p + T_s} \end{aligned}

    R is Rs or Rp of one surface. Reflected light: R′ = 1 − T and P = (R′s − R′p)/(R′s + R′p).

  2. Malus's law

    I = I_0\cos^2\theta

    Unpolarized light: I = I₀/2 after the first polarizer, because the average of cos²θ is ½.

  3. Turning polarizer between crossed polarizers

    \begin{aligned} \theta &= \omega t = 2\pi f t \\[5pt] I &= I_1\cos^2\theta\,\sin^2\theta \\[5pt] &= \frac{I_1}{4}\,\sin^2 2\theta \\[5pt] &= \frac{I_1}{8}\,\bigl(1 - \cos 4\omega t\bigr) \end{aligned}

    I₁ is the intensity after the first polarizer; the output oscillates at 4f, four maxima per turn.

  4. Real polarizers (Jones matrix)

    \begin{aligned} \mathbf J(\theta) &= \hat{\mathbf p}\,\hat{\mathbf p}^{\mathsf T} + \sqrt{\varepsilon}\;\hat{\mathbf b}\,\hat{\mathbf b}^{\mathsf T} \\[5pt] \hat{\mathbf p} &= (\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta) \\[5pt] \hat{\mathbf b} &= (-\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta) \end{aligned}

    Components along vertical and horizontal; ε is the intensity that leaks through for light polarized across the axis. Crossed real polarizers pass about εI₀ of unpolarized light I₀ (εI₀/2 by each of two paths).
